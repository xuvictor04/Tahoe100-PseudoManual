> [!summary] In one line
> FUS is an abundant, essential RNA- and DNA-binding protein involved in transcription, splicing and the DNA damage response, mutated in familial ALS and rearranged in several sarcomas — and, in this study, the highly expressed gene lying 17.05 kb 5′ of TRIM72 on the same strand.

## What it is

FUS (fused in sarcoma, also TLS) is the founding member of the FET family alongside EWSR1 and TAF15. Its architecture is a list of interaction modules rather than an enzyme: an N-terminal QGSY-rich low-complexity prion-like domain, RGG repeats, an RRM, a zinc finger, and a C-terminal PY-type nuclear localisation signal read by Transportin-1/karyopherin-β2. It is predominantly nuclear but shuttles, and it binds thousands of transcripts with loose specificity. Functionally it has been placed at transcription — interacting with the RNA polymerase II C-terminal domain and affecting initiation and elongation — at alternative splicing, at mRNA transport, and in paraspeckles together with NEAT1. It also participates in the DNA damage response, being recruited rapidly to damage sites in a PARP- and poly(ADP-ribose)-dependent manner, with reported roles in double-strand break repair and in ligation during oxidative damage repair.

The low-complexity domain makes FUS a textbook phase-separating protein, and the same property that lets it condense functionally lets it solidify pathologically: purified FUS undergoes a liquid-to-solid transition that disease mutations accelerate. Clinically FUS is best known from two directions. Mutations cause ALS type 6 and some frontotemporal dementia; most cluster in the PY-NLS, weaken Transportin binding, and drive cytoplasmic mislocalisation and inclusion formation, so the disease is usually read as a mislocalisation disease rather than a simple loss of function. Separately, chromosomal translocations fuse the FUS N-terminal low-complexity domain to DNA-binding partners — FUS–DDIT3 (CHOP) in myxoid liposarcoma, FUS–ERG in leukaemia, FUS–CREB3L2 in low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma — where it acts as a potent transactivation module. FUS is highly expressed in essentially all proliferating cells.

## Why it appears in this work

Position, first and foremost. FUS is the dominant donor in [[The TRIM72 locus]]: same strand, 17.05 kb 5′ of TRIM72, and running at roughly 47× TRIM72's level in these pools. Because [[Readthrough spillover]] scales with the donor's expression and not the recipient's, a small proportional failure of termination at FUS is a large proportional gain for the region annotated as TRIM72. That geometry is the main reason behind [[Why MG53 is hard to measure here]], and it is why FUS is read in this vault first as a measurement covariate and only second as a gene. Its own biology reinforces the concern rather than relieving it: readthrough rises under stress and under transcription and translation inhibitors, and FUS is both heavily expressed and heavily transcribed.

## What is uncertain

Whether FUS is a quantitatively sufficient donor is not established. The strand-aware evidence that plus-strand readthrough is really occurring at this locus is solid, but the flux arithmetic calibrated on it over-predicts the observed rise, so "FUS readthrough explains the MG53 signal" overstates what has been shown — the honest form is that it is the leading account and the numbers do not yet close. Whether changes in FUS's own counts are pathway responses is also unsettled: FUS is not a known p53 target and has no established p53 response element, so movement in FUS more plausibly tracks global transcriptional output or termination fidelity than any specific signal. And nothing about FUS's protein-level biology — condensation state, localisation, DDR recruitment — is visible in mRNA, so a change in FUS counts says nothing about whether FUS is functioning normally in these cells.

## References

- Crozat et al., 1993, Nature — identified FUS/TLS through its fusion to CHOP in human myxoid liposarcoma.
- Kwiatkowski et al., 2009, Science — found mutations in FUS/TLS on chromosome 16 causing familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.
- Vance et al., 2009, Science — independently identified FUS mutations as the cause of familial ALS type 6.
- Patel et al., 2015, Cell — showed a liquid-to-solid phase transition of FUS that is accelerated by disease mutation.
- Mastrocola et al., 2013, Journal of Biological Chemistry — showed FUS acts downstream of PARP in the response to DNA damage, with recruitment dependent on poly(ADP-ribose).
